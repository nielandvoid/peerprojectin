The Peer Project // Extracurriculars

Things to do with your time that are not studying, from volunteering to building things. If a programme has an age or class limit, it's in the description.

blogs are actually good

Putting up a website of your own is actually really easy, and it costs nothing. GitHub Pages hosts a simple site for free. Even this website is hosted there at no cost. Cloudflare Pages, Netlify and Vercel have free plans that do the same job. One page of text is already a website. If you get stuck, an AI assistant is good at exactly this part: ask it to set up a simple page and walk you through putting it online, then change the words yourself.

If you would rather not touch any of that, platforms like Medium, Substack and Blogger are always there. You sign up, you write, and it is published. You can move to your own site later, and what you wrote comes with you.

The point is, ideas slip away. Many things cross your mind in a week: a question you could not answer, a way of solving a problem that finally clicked, something you noticed on the way to school. Most of them are gone by evening, and a blog is a place to preserve a few before they go. Think of it like catching wild pokemons.

Write whatever you feel like writing about. It does not have to be new or important. It is fair to think that in a world already full of information, one more explanation of something is of no use to anyone, and it is true that a lot of it will be read by nobody. But writing something down is how you find out whether you actually understood it, and the person it helps first is you, a year from now. Some days it will also be someone else who had the same question you did and could not find it explained plainly.

Use an AI to help you build the page if you like, but write the posts yourself. The point of the exercise is your own thinking, and that part cannot be handed over.

volunteering and NGOs

Most volunteering programmes have a minimum age, which is the thing nobody tells you until you have filled in the form. We have noted it wherever we could check it.

Online

ZooniverseOnline

Real research projects you can contribute to from your phone or laptop, by classifying images or transcribing records. You can do as much or as little as you like. Click "Projects" in the top menu and filter by subject, such as space, nature or history, to find one.

UNICEF U-ReportOnline

UNICEF's platform for young people, with an India edition that you join through WhatsApp, Messenger or Telegram. You answer polls on issues that matter to young people, and the results go to the people who make decisions. You need to be 13 or older.

Smithsonian Transcription CenterOnline

Transcribe handwritten historical documents and field notes from home. Registered volunteers must be 14 or older, but you can transcribe anonymously without an account.

Schoolhouse.worldOnline

Teach other students for free, over live online sessions. Tutors are trained volunteers, many of them students. The requirements for tutoring are on the site.

StoryWeaver (Pratham Books)Online

An open library of children's stories, where volunteers translate stories into Indian languages. We found no stated age rule, so write to them and ask.

UN Volunteers (online)Online

Online assignments with UN agencies and partners. You must be at least 18 to sign up, so this one is for later. Each assignment sets its own requirements.

In person

GoonjIn person

Works on clothing, disaster relief and menstrual hygiene, and runs collection drives and school sessions that volunteers organise. Their FAQ says anyone can volunteer, whatever their age, and their volunteer page lists drives you can run at your own school.

Make A DifferenceIn person

A volunteer-led mentoring programme in 19 cities, where you mentor a child through school and help build their confidence. You need to commit 3-4 hours every week, show up consistently, and stay for a minimum of one year. To start, register for a MADster Workshop in your city. They do not state a minimum age, so check with them before you apply.

National Service Scheme (NSS)In person

The government's community service scheme for Class 11 and 12 and college students, run through your school. Expect roughly 120 hours of service a year plus a camp. Ask your school whether it has a unit.

Bharat Scouts and GuidesIn person

India's scouting and guiding association, run through schools and state associations. Membership rules and fees vary by state.

Junior Red CrossIn person

The school wing of the Indian Red Cross Society, run through state branches. The age and class rules differ by state, so ask your school or your district branch.

Pratham Youth Summer ProgrammeIn person

A summer programme with Pratham's education work in Aurangabad, open to students aged 16 to 19. The 2025 edition ran in July. Dates and eligibility change each year, so check the current one.

NCC Junior DivisionIn person

The National Cadet Corps for school students, roughly Classes 8 to 10 and ages 12 to 18. You join through your school. If it has no unit, you may be able to join through Open NCC. Medical standards apply.

clubs

This is our own advice, not an official procedure. Schools differ a lot, so adjust it to yours.

  1. Decide what members will do in the first three meetings. If you cannot answer that, the idea is not ready.
  2. Find two or three friends who will actually turn up. A club of three that meets is better than a club of thirty that does not.
  3. Find one teacher who will sponsor it. Most schools need a member of staff's name on a club.
  4. Write a one-page proposal: what it is for, who can join, what you will do, how often and where you will meet, what it costs (usually nothing), and what you need from the school.
  5. Ask for a pilot. "One term, then we review" is an easier yes than a permanent club.
  6. Keep a record: who came, what you did, photos. That is your case for continuing it, and what the next batch of juniors inherits.

If your school has no system for clubs at all, propose the system, not just your club. A teacher in charge, a one-page form and a slot on the notice board is enough, and you can offer to draft it. A principal can say yes to a simple process much more easily than to an open-ended "let us have clubs".

Interact clubs (Rotary)In person

A ready-made club structure for ages 12 to 18, with a service focus. It runs with a sponsoring Rotary club, so you get a mentor and a framework instead of starting from nothing.

Girl UpIn person

Girl Up has an India chapter, with clubs in schools, colleges and communities across the country. You can start a club or contact the India team to join an existing one.

school magazine / student council

These two already exist in most schools, so you do not have to build anything. You join, then you take on more than the job asks for. That is usually what separates a name on a list from something you can describe properly later.

School magazine

A magazine is a small publishing operation: someone commissions pieces, someone writes, someone edits, someone lays it out, and someone makes sure it comes out on the date it was promised. Most students only ever try the writing part. The rest is where you learn the most.

Start by writing for it. A short, specific piece that is finished and clean is worth more than an ambitious one that never arrives. Then ask to edit, because reading other people's drafts and telling them what is unclear teaches you more about writing than your own drafts do. If your school has no magazine, you can start one: a single printed or shared-document issue per term, with a teacher who signs off on it, is enough, and the club steps above apply here too. Keep your published work in one place, with dates. It becomes a portfolio, and something concrete to point to on an application.

Student council

A council is not mainly about the title. It is the one place in a school where students are expected to represent other students to the people who run it, and that is a skill most adults are still working on.

Run on one or two things you can actually change, not a long list. "Cleaner water coolers on the second floor" is a better platform than "a better school". Once elected, find out what the council is allowed to decide and what it can only suggest, since much of the frustration in a council comes from not knowing this at the start. Gather what students want before you speak for them; a short form, or ten minutes in each class, is enough. Write down what you asked for and what happened, including what was refused, because that record is useful for whoever comes after you. If you lose the election, the work is still available: committees, house teams and event crews are usually short of people.

On a profile, both of these are judged on what you did, not on the title. "Edited four issues and introduced a student-writing section" tells someone far more than "editor".

model UN

Model UN is a simulation of the United Nations. You are given a country and an agenda, and you debate and negotiate as that country's delegate. The real skill is not speaking. It is understanding a position that may not be your own well enough to argue for it, and then working out a deal with people who disagree with you.

Before a conference, read about your country's actual policy on the agenda, not what you would do in its place. Delegates who do this stand out immediately. Prepare a short opening speech, and a position paper if the conference asks for one, but expect most of the rest to happen in informal discussion, so be ready to listen as much as you talk. Treat your first conference as practice: speak at least once, even if it is short, and watch how experienced delegates build support for a resolution.

School, inter-school and university-run conferences all exist, and they differ in size and quality. Many charge a registration fee, and some charge a lot, so check the fee and what it covers before committing. If your school has no MUN club, the club steps above apply, and a school can host its own small conference for its own students before it ever sends anyone outside.

olympiads

Firstly, about SOF (IMO, NSO, IEO and the rest)

sofworld.org

Schools register whole classes for these, they are open to Classes 1 to 12, and the entry fee is small. They are fine as a first look at competitive exams, but they are not significant on a profile. SOF is a private organisation, entry is open to everyone with no qualifying round, and it has no link to the route that picks India's teams for the international olympiads. A good SOF rank does not count towards RMO, INMO or the olympiads below, so do not present it as the same thing.

The ones that do count are run by HBCSE. The stage-by-stage exams and the books worth using are on the subject pages.

Physics

NSEP, INPhO and IPhO, with the books in the order to read them.

Mathematics

RMO, INMO and IMO.

Chemistry

NSEC, INChO and IChO.

Biology

NSEB, INBO and IBO.

Indian Computing Olympiad (IARCS)

The route into competitive programming for school students in India. The Olympiad section of the IARCS site has the current dates and past papers.

HBCSE Olympiads

The body that runs the national stage for science and maths, including astronomy, which we have not covered on its own page.

competitions and tournaments

Large, structured competitions with a clear ladder from school to national level, the same shape as an olympiad but for sport and the arts.

CBSE sports: clusters, zonals and nationalsIn person

CBSE organises sports at cluster and zonal level, then a national level, in age groups for girls and boys separately. Your school registers you. For 2026-27 the tentative windows are June to July for clusters and zonals and September to October for nationals. Who advances differs by sport, so read the current circular.

Kala UtsavIn person

The Ministry of Education's national arts competition for Classes IX to XII: vocal and instrumental music, dance, visual arts, drama and more, going from school to district to state to national. You enter one art form. The official site has the current guidelines.

Shankar's International Children's CompetitionIn person

One of the oldest children's painting and writing competitions in the world, run since 1949 by Children's Book Trust. There is no entry fee and there are age categories, with the on-the-spot painting event listed for 25 October 2026 in New Delhi. Age rules differ between sources, so check the current ones.

building stuff

Hack ClubOnline

A nonprofit community of teenagers, aged 13 to 18, who make things together. Good if you want people to build with, not just tutorials to follow.

High school hackathons (Hack Club directory)Online and in person

A running list of hackathons for high school students, with a filter for online events and a section for the Asia-Pacific region, which has included events in India. It also tells you how to run one yourself. On the page, use the format filter for online events or open the Asia-Pacific section, and enter your email to be told when new events are added near you. Check each event's own rules for age and entry.

Code Club and CoderDojoOnline

Free, volunteer-run coding clubs from the Raspberry Pi Foundation, for ages 9 to 13 (Code Club) and 7 to 17 (CoderDojo). We found no confirmed clubs in India, so you may need to start one, which is what the clubs section above is for.

Girls Who CodeOnline

Free computer science clubs for girls from Class 3 to 12. They announced an India launch in 2019, but we could not confirm current clubs, so check before you plan around it.

Technovation GirlsOnline

A free programme where teams of girls aged 8 to 18 build an app or an AI solution for a problem in their community, with mentors. Open to girls and gender-diverse participants, from anywhere. Registration usually opens around August each year, so check the current season.

CS50xOnline

Harvard's free introduction to computer science, and a good first course if you have never programmed. The problem sets are the real learning, so do them. CS50 is the Harvard course, and CS50x is its free online version for everyone, which is the one linked here. Harvard also has separate, more focused CS50 courses on Python, the web, AI, SQL, cybersecurity and more, listed on the same site. CS50x is the broad introduction, so start there unless you already know you want one of the narrower ones. There is a free CS50 certificate, which you get by scoring at least 70% on every problem set and the final project (open "CS50 Certificate" in the site menu). The paid verified certificate is through edX at cs50.edx.org. It is not worth paying for unless something specific asks for it. Admissions and employers care far more about what you built with the course than about a certificate, and the free one already shows you did the work.

freeCodeCampOnline

Free, project-based web development courses. Pick the thing you want to build, then take the course that gets you there.

online courses (Udemy, Coursera and the rest)

Certificates from these usually carry little weight. They show that you finished a video course, not that anyone tested what you can do, and they do not appear on your school transcript. They are still something: they show that you went looking for things to learn, and they are worth more when you use them. Finish the course, then build something with it, enter a competition, or write about what you learned. Pay for a certificate only if you need one for a specific reason.

CourseraOnline

Courses from universities and companies. You can audit most of them for free, which gives you the lectures and readings. The certificate and graded work need payment or an approved financial aid application.

edXOnline

The same model: auditing is free and verified certificates are paid. CS50x above runs here.

NPTEL (SWAYAM)Online

Courses from the IITs and IISc that are free to learn from. The proctored exam and certificate are optional and cost roughly ₹1,000 per course, and no age limit or entry requirement is stated. Check the current fee on SWAYAM.

UdemyOnline

Paid courses by individual instructors, with frequent sales that bring most of them down to a small price. Quality varies a lot, so check the ratings and the preview before you buy. Its certificate is a completion record from Udemy, and it counts for the least of these.

cybersec / hacking

TryHackMeOnline

Guided, hands-on cybersecurity lessons in the browser, from the basics up. You must be 13 or older, and some content and community features are restricted until you are 18.

picoCTF (CMU CyLab Security Academy)Online

Carnegie Mellon's free capture-the-flag competition and learning platform for school students, now part of its CyLab Security Academy. Anyone can practise. Existing picoCTF logins still work, and you sign in through cylabacademy.org. The 2025 competition asked for 13 or older, with parental consent under 18, so check the current rules before you enter.

TJCTFOnline

A CTF run by students at Thomas Jefferson High School in the US, aimed at high schoolers. It is international, and teams can have up to five players. The most recent edition ran 15 to 17 May 2026, so watch for next year's.

CTFtimeOnline

The calendar of CTF competitions worldwide. Most are online and free, and you register on each organiser's own site. Open an event's page and follow its official link, and read the rules there to see whether school students can enter. Registration is not on CTFtime.

OverTheWireOnline

Free wargames you play from a terminal at any time of year, with no event dates. Start with Bandit, and begin at Level 0 from the menu on the left of its page. You connect over SSH from a terminal, so you will need one on your computer. It teaches the command line as you go.

CSAWIn person

NYU's cybersecurity competitions, with an India region run with IIT Kanpur. CSAW 2026 is on 12 to 14 November. Its participants range from high school to doctoral students, so check the rules for whether a school-level entry is open to you.

Hack The BoxOnline

Hands-on hacking labs and a CTF community. Under-18s need a parent or guardian to complete a consent form before the account is approved, so allow time for that.

research and science projects

Journal of Emerging Investigators

A journal for pre-college students, with scientist reviewers. The submission guidelines show what a publishable school project looks like, which is worth reading even if you never submit.

INSPIRE-MANAKIn person

The Department of Science and Technology's scheme for school students' ideas and projects. Nominations come through the school, so ask your science teacher.

and more

Debate, sports and music are all worth a look. We have not vetted specific organisers for these, so start by asking your school what already exists, then look for conferences and competitions near you.
